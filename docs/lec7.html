Lecture 7: Integration of Functions & ODEs

The one-sentence version: integration is prediction — "given how things change, where will they be?" — and the craft is spending function evaluations where they buy accuracy: higher-order rules, adaptive steps, and never letting a dead fast mode dictate your step size.

The big picture

Lecture 3 interpolated between samples; this lecture sums those interpolants. Every quadrature rule below is literally "fit a Lecture-3 polynomial on each panel, integrate it exactly" — and every ODE method is "use the slope now to predict the state next".

1 · Why robots integrate

The slides frame \(I = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) as the ODE \(y' = f(x)\), \(y(a) = 0\), solve for \(y(b)\) — so quadrature and ODE solving are one subject. Applications: position estimation, path optimization, image restoration. The goal everywhere: a good estimate of y(b) with a reasonable number of evaluations.

2 · Trapezoid, Simpson & friends

Sample uniformly, \(x_i = x_0 + ih\), \(f_i = f(x_i)\), and integrate the interpolant (the Newton–Cotes family):

$$\text{trapezoid: } \int_{x_0}^{x_1} f \approx \frac{f_0 + f_1}{2}h + O(f''h^3) \qquad \text{Simpson: } \int_{x_0}^{x_2} f \approx \frac{h}{3}(f_0 + 4f_1 + f_2) + O(h^5 f^{(4)})$$
rulefits a…per-panel errorwhole-interval order
trapezoidchord (degree 1)O(h³f″)error ∝ 1/N² — doubling N buys ÷4
midpointconstant at the centerO(h³f″), half the trapezoid's constant∝ 1/N²; never evaluates the endpoints (handy for singular ends)
Simpsonparabola (degree 2)O(h⁵f⁗) — exact through degree 3∝ 1/N⁴ — doubling N buys ÷16
Simpson ⅜cubic over 3 panels\(\tfrac{h}{8}(3f_0+9f_1+9f_2+3f_3)\)… see fine printsame order as Simpson

Chained over N panels, the extended trapezoid is \(h(\tfrac12 f_0 + f_1 + \dots + f_{N-1} + \tfrac12 f_N)\) with error \(O\!\big((x_N - x_0)^3 f'' / N^2\big)\).

💡 The lucky accident of Simpson: a parabola (degree 2) integrates cubics exactly too — the cubic's extra error is odd and cancels over the symmetric panel. That free degree is where the jump from h³ to h⁵ comes from.

🕹️ Try it: watch the shaded panels hug the curve, and the error divide by 4 (trapezoid) or 16 (Simpson) per doubling. The spike preset shows uniform panels failing.

Worked example: ∫₀^π sin x dx = 2, verified orders (click to open)

Trapezoid error at N = 16: 6.4×10⁻³; at N = 32: 1.6×10⁻³ → ratio 4.00 ✓ (order 2). Simpson: 1.66×10⁻⁵ → 1.03×10⁻⁶ → ratio 16.06 ✓ (order 4). Midpoint at N = 16: 3.2×10⁻³ — same order as trapezoid, about half the error, opposite sign.

3 · Romberg: coarse-to-fine, then extrapolate to h = 0

The slides' "strategy": refine coarse-to-fine and watch convergence. The payoff is the Euler–Maclaurin formula — the trapezoid's error is a clean series in even powers of h, with Bernoulli-number coefficients (\(B_0 = 1, B_2 = \tfrac16, B_4 = -\tfrac1{30}\), from \(t/(e^t-1) = \sum B_n t^n / n!\)). A series in h² can be extrapolated to h = 0: combine the N and 2N trapezoid answers to cancel the h² term, then those to cancel h⁴, and so on — reusing every evaluation already made.

Worked example: the Romberg table for ∫₀^π sin (click to open)

Errors down the first column (plain trapezoid, N = 2…32): 4.3×10⁻¹ → 1.6×10⁻³. Along the extrapolated diagonal: 4.3×10⁻¹ → 4.6×10⁻³ → 1.7×10⁻⁵ → 1.6×10⁻⁸ → 4×10⁻¹². Same 33 samples, eight more digits. ✓ verified.

4 · Improper integrals: fix them by substitution

The slides' taxonomy of "improper": a removable point value (\(\sin x / x\) at 0), an infinite limit, an endpoint singularity (\(x^{-1/2}\) at 0), an interior singularity (known or unknown location). If the value is infinite (\(\int_0^\infty x^{-1}\)), it's not improper — it's impossible.

The cure is a change of variable. For an infinite limit: \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \int_{1/b}^{1/a} \tfrac{1}{t^2} f(1/t)\,dt\) (valid when a, b share a sign). In general \(\int f(x)dx = \int f(x(t))\,\frac{dx}{dt}dt\) — pick x(t) so the transformed integrand is tame. The slides' example, the Schwartz tanh rule:

$$x = \tfrac12(b+a) + \tfrac12(b-a)\tanh t, \qquad \frac{dx}{dt} = \tfrac12(b-a)\,\mathrm{sech}^2 t$$

sech² dies double-exponentially fast, crushing endpoint singularities before they hurt. Verified: \(\int_0^1 x^{-1/2}dx = 2\); naive trapezoid hits 1/√0 = ∞ at the first endpoint, the tanh-transformed trapezoid quietly returns 1.995 with 201 points (and converges further with a wider t-window).

5 · Gauss integration & adaptivity

Drop uniform spacing: choose nodes and weights, \(\int_a^b W(x) f(x) dx \approx \sum_j W_j f(x_j)\). With n optimally placed nodes you integrate polynomials up to degree 2n−1 exactly — double the Newton–Cotes order for the same evaluations. (The nodes turn out to be roots of orthogonal polynomials — Chebyshev's cousins from Lecture 4.) The slides also note the mixture view: approximate f by Gaussians and integrate those in closed form.

💡 Partitioned/adaptive integration: when dynamics vary across the interval ("Rule 1 of data analysis: understand your data"), split \(\int_a^b = \int_a^m + \int_m^b\) and refine each part until it converges — spend panels where the function moves. That's what the spike preset in the demo is begging for.

6 · ODEs: everything becomes first order

Any higher-order ODE reduces to coupled first-order ones by naming the derivatives. The slides' mass under force F:

$$m\ddot u_1 = F(u_1) \;\xrightarrow{\;u_2 \,=\, \dot u_1\;}\; \frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}u_1\\u_2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}u_2\\ F(u_1)/m\end{pmatrix} \;=\; f(u, t)$$

So every solver below only ever sees \(\dot y = f(y, t)\) — state in, slope out. (That state vector is exactly a robot's \((\theta, \dot\theta)\).) Three families: Runge–Kutta (the generic workhorse, great when f is cheap), Richardson/Bulirsch–Stoer (high precision), predictor-corrector (historically interesting, rarely used today).

7 · Euler → RK2 → RK4

Forward Euler trusts the slope at the start of the step for the whole step:

$$y_{n+1} = y_n + h f(x_n, y_n) \qquad \text{(asymmetric: error } O(h^2) \text{ per step)}$$

RK2 fixes the asymmetry by probing the midpoint first; RK4 probes four times and takes a weighted average:

$$\begin{aligned} k_1 &= hf(x_n, y_n) & k_2 &= hf(x_n + \tfrac h2, y_n + \tfrac{k_1}2)\\ k_3 &= hf(x_n + \tfrac h2, y_n + \tfrac{k_2}2) & k_4 &= hf(x_n + h, y_n + k_3) \end{aligned} \qquad y_{n+1} = y_n + \tfrac{k_1}6 + \tfrac{k_2}3 + \tfrac{k_3}3 + \tfrac{k_4}6 + O(h^5)$$
Worked example: measured orders on y′ = y (click to open)

Error at x = 1 (true value e), halving h from 0.05 to 0.025: Euler 6.5×10⁻² → 3.3×10⁻² (÷1.96 ≈ 2¹), RK2 1.1×10⁻³ → 2.8×10⁻⁴ (÷3.93 ≈ 2²), RK4 1.4×10⁻⁷ → 8.7×10⁻⁹ (÷15.7 ≈ 2⁴). Orders 1, 2, 4 ✓.

🕹️ Try it: same pendulum, same step — Euler spirals outward (energy leak), RK4's orbit closes. Crank h up and watch Euler fling the pendulum over the top.

8 · Adaptive step size & PI control

The obvious error estimate (slides): take a full step, take two half steps, compare; recurse if needed. Nobody goes beyond 5th-order RK — better to spend effort on step-size control than on order. And since step control is a feedback loop, the slides close it with an actual PI controller:

$$h_{n+1} = S\,h_n\,\mathrm{err}_n^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{err}_{n-1}^{\beta}, \qquad \alpha = \tfrac1k - 0.75\beta, \quad \beta = \tfrac{0.4}{k}$$

S a safety factor, k the integrator's order. The proportional part reacts to the current error, the integral part smooths against oscillating step sizes — control theory applied to the solver itself.

9 · Bulirsch–Stoer: midpoints + Richardson extrapolation

Take one big step H, crossed by n modified-midpoint substeps (h = H/n, centered differences, so the error is a series in even powers of h — same trick as Romberg):

$$z_0 = y(x_n), \quad z_1 = z_0 + hf, \quad z_{m+1} = z_{m-1} + 2hf(x_n + mh, z_m), \quad y(x_n + H) = \tfrac12\big[z_n + z_{n-1} + hf(x+H, z_n)\big]$$

Then combine runs with different n and extrapolate to h → 0. The first rung:

$$y(x+H) = \frac{4y_n - y_{n/2}}{3} \quad\text{— 4th order, like RK4, with ~⅔ fewer evaluations}$$

Deeper rungs use the Romberg-style tableau (each new n adds a row; \(T_{kk} - T_{k,k-1}\) is a free error estimate, driving step-size selection). Step sequences: n = 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32… or the newer nj = 2(j+1).

Worked example: the first extrapolation really is 4th order (click to open)

y′ = y over H = 1: modified midpoint with n = 4 errs by 2.7×10⁻², n = 8 by 7.0×10⁻³ (÷3.9 — the h² series at work). Richardson: (4y₈ − y₄)/3 errs by 3.6×10⁻⁴, a 20× bonus with zero new evaluations. ✓

⚠️ When not to use it (slides): functions with table lookups / interpolated data (not smooth enough to extrapolate), singularities inside the range, or "expensive" f. Bulirsch–Stoer is the precision tool for smooth problems; RK is the generic one.

10 · Stiffness: the dead mode that owns your step size

The slides' "variable dynamics" example:

$$u' = 998u + 1998v, \qquad v' = -999u - 1999v, \qquad u(0)=1,\; v(0)=0$$ $$\Rightarrow\; u = 2e^{-x} - e^{-1000x}, \qquad v = -e^{-x} + e^{-1000x}$$

Two time scales: eigenvalues −1 and −1000 (verified). The fast term is numerically zero after x ≈ 0.01 — but explicit Euler's stability requires \(|1 + h\lambda| \le 1\) for every eigenvalue, so h ≤ 2/1000 = 0.002 forever.

🕹️ Try it: slide h across 0.002 and watch a perfectly smooth solution detonate. The cliff is exactly where |1 − 1000h| crosses 1.

💡 Why robots care: stiffness is everywhere a fast subsystem rides on a slow one — motor electrical dynamics (ms) under arm mechanics (s), contact forces under body motion. Remedies: implicit methods (backward Euler is stable at any h) or splitting the time scales. Diagnosing "my sim explodes unless h is tiny" as stiffness is a rite of passage.

11 · PDEs in one slide

When the unknown is a field u(x, t), the three canonical families (whole courses exist: MATH 110/231):

typeequationprototypeproblem flavor
hyperbolic\(u_{tt} = v^2 u_{xx}\)waveinitial-value: march forward in time
parabolic\(u_t = \partial_x(D\,u_x)\)diffusioninitial-value, smoothing
elliptic\(u_{xx} + u_{yy} = \rho(x,y)\)Poissonboundary-value: solve everywhere at once

Finite differences on a grid turn all three into (sparse) linear algebra — initial-value problems march like ODEs; boundary-value problems become one big Lecture-2 solve. Robotics sightings: potential-field planners solve Poisson-like problems; mapping/diffusion models are parabolic.

12 · Summary

  • Quadrature = integrate the interpolant: chords give order 2, parabolas order 4, and symmetric rules get a free extra degree.
  • Smooth error series ⇒ extrapolate to h = 0: Romberg (integrals) and Bulirsch–Stoer (ODEs) get many digits from few evaluations.
  • Improper ≠ impossible — substitute until the integrand is tame (x → 1/t, tanh rule).
  • Any ODE → first-order system. RK4 is the default; adaptive step control (even PI-controlled) is where the real efficiency lives.
  • Stiffness: stability, not accuracy, limits h — a decayed fast mode still constrains every explicit step. Go implicit.

🧠 Self-check questions
1 · Simpson fits parabolas. Why is it exact for cubics too?
Over a symmetric panel the cubic term's error is an odd function around the center — it integrates to zero. Symmetry buys one free degree; that's the h⁵ in the error.
2 · Romberg and Bulirsch–Stoer share one trick. What is it, and what does it require?
The error is a clean series in h² (Euler–Maclaurin for trapezoid; centered midpoint for ODEs), so answers at several h can be extrapolated to h = 0. It requires smoothness — tables, kinks, and singularities break the series, which is exactly the slides' warning list for B-S.
3 · Why does forward Euler make the pendulum gain energy rather than lose it?
Each step moves along the tangent of the circular orbit — a chord that lands slightly outside the circle, every single step. The radius (energy) can only ratchet up. RK4's probes cancel that bias to 4th order.
4 · Your drone sim is stable at h = 1 ms but explodes at 2.5 ms, long after all transients die. Stiff or inaccurate?
Stiff: explosion at a sharp h threshold independent of visible dynamics is a stability cliff (some fast mode has |1+hλ| crossing 1), not an accuracy problem. Accuracy degrades gradually; stability fails totally. Switch to an implicit integrator or shrink h.
5 · ∫₀¹ x^(−1/2) dx: why does the midpoint rule survive where the trapezoid dies, and what's the better fix?
Midpoint never evaluates the endpoints, so it never touches 1/√0 — it converges, slowly. The better fix is the tanh (or 1/t) substitution, which makes the transformed integrand smooth and gives fast convergence.
6 · Why is step-size control described as a PI controller — what are the "P" and "I" reacting to?
P: the current step's error estimate (err_nᵅ) — react now. I: the previous error (err_{n−1}^β) — a memory that damps oscillating grow/shrink cycles in h. The solver is literally a plant being controlled.
