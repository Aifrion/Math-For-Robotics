Lecture 6: Optimization

The one-sentence version: most of robotics is "make this number small" — and the methods split by what you can afford: compare values (golden section, simplex), follow slopes (Powell, conjugate gradient), gamble (simulated annealing), or exhaust a graph (dynamic programming).

The big picture

Lecture 5 solved f(x) = 0. Setting the derivative to zero turns that machinery into minimization — but this lecture mostly avoids derivatives entirely, because robot objective functions (simulation rollouts, collision costs, sensor models) often don't come with them. Minimum vs maximum is immaterial: flip the sign of f.

1 · Robotics is optimization

The slides' examples: best high-speed trajectory, best model fit to a data swarm, optimal coverage for fire monitoring, energy-efficient San Diego → Hawaii travel, and path planning on a potential field (the red route snaking between cost mountains). Different costumes, one question: which input makes this function smallest? Watch for two recurring villains: local minima and narrow curved valleys.

2 · 1-D first: bracketing an extremum

A root needs 2 points (a sign change). A minimum needs 3: \(a < b < c\) with \(f(b) < f(a)\) and \(f(b) < f(c)\) — then a minimum is trapped in \([a, c]\).

Where to probe next? Demand that both possible outcomes shrink the bracket by the same factor, and that the ratios inside stay the same forever (self-similarity). With \(w = \frac{b-a}{c-a}\) that gives:

$$w^2 - 3w + 1 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; w = \frac{3 - \sqrt 5}{2} \approx 0.38197$$
symbolmeaning
\(w \approx 0.382\)the short fraction of the bracket — where b sits, and where the probe goes in the long half
\(1 - w \approx 0.618\)bracket shrink factor per evaluation (the golden ratio conjugate)
Worked example: how fast is 0.618 per step? (click to open)

40 evaluations on \((x-2)^2 - 10\) starting from bracket [−2, 10]: final bracket width \(12 \times 0.618^{40} \approx 4 \times 10^{-8}\), b lands on 2.0000000 ✓. Like bisection, it's linear convergence — reliable, indifferent to the function, never fast.

3 · Parabolic interpolation: Brent, minimization edition

Through 3 points there is one parabola; jump to its vertex:

$$x = b - \frac12\,\frac{(b-a)^2[f(b)-f(c)] - (b-c)^2[f(b)-f(a)]}{(b-a)[f(b)-f(c)] - (b-c)[f(b)-f(a)]}$$

On a quadratic it lands exactly in one step (verified: from (0, 1, 4) on \((x-2)^2-10\) it returns 2.0). When does it fail? When the three points are collinear — the denominator goes to zero and the "parabola" is a line with no vertex. The remedy is the same Brent pattern as Lecture 5: try the parabolic jump, fall back to golden section when it's degenerate or lands outside. With \(f'(b)\) available, the derivative's sign picks which half to search — same bracket, better information.

4 · Downhill simplex (Nelder–Mead): no gradients, no model

In N dimensions, take N+1 points (a triangle in 2-D, tetrahedron in 3-D). One move per iteration, tried in order — sort vertices so \(f(x_1) \le \dots \le f(x_{N+1})\), let \(\bar x\) be the centroid of all but the worst:

$$\text{reflect: } x_r = \bar x + \alpha(\bar x - x_{N+1}) \quad\to\quad \text{expand } (\gamma) \;/\; \text{contract } (\rho) \;/\; \text{shrink } (\sigma)$$
movewhenstandard coefficient
reflectionalways try first: flip the worst vertex through the centroidα = 1
expansionreflection beat the best point → go furtherγ = 2
contractionreflection still worst-ish → pull inwardρ = 0.5
shrinkeven contraction failed → collapse toward the best vertexσ = 0.5

🕹️ Try it: step the triangle across three landscapes. two valleys shows the local-minimum trap that motivates Section 7.

Verified: this exact algorithm drives Rosenbrock's banana from (−1.2, 1) to the minimum (1, 1) — slowly, but with zero derivative evaluations. It's scipy.optimize.minimize(method='Nelder-Mead').

5 · Powell's method: line searches in smart directions

Near a minimum every smooth function looks quadratic:

$$f(\vec x) \approx c - \vec b \cdot \vec x + \tfrac12 \vec x^T A \vec x, \qquad A_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \partial x_j} \;\;(\text{the Hessian}), \qquad \nabla f = A\vec x - \vec b$$

Powell's recipe: keep N directions \(u_i\) (initially the coordinate axes). Minimize along each in turn (a 1-D search from Section 2–3 each time), then replace one direction with the overall displacement \(P_n - P_0\) — the "average" direction that actually helped — and repeat. On a quadratic, the directions become mutually conjugate (\(u_i^T A\, u_j = 0\)), which makes successive line searches stop undoing each other.

⚠️ The slides' caveat: "might generate linearly degenerate solutions" — the direction set can collapse into a subspace and stop exploring some dimension entirely. Practical fixes: periodically reset to the coordinate axes, or discard the direction of largest decrease instead (Numerical Recipes' version).

6 · Steepest descent zigzags; conjugate gradient doesn't

With gradients available, the obvious move is downhill: \(P_{i+1} = P_i - \lambda \nabla f(P_i)\). With exact line search each step is perpendicular to the last — in a stretched valley that means zigzagging, paying for the same wall over and over. Conjugate gradient fixes the direction, not the step size:

$$g_{i+1} = g_i - \lambda_i A h_i, \qquad h_{i+1} = g_{i+1} + \gamma_i h_i, \qquad \lambda_i = \frac{g_i^T g_i}{h_i^T A h_i}, \qquad \gamma_i = \frac{g_{i+1}^T g_{i+1}}{g_i^T g_i}$$
symbolmeaning
\(g_i\)current (negative) gradient — the raw "downhill"
\(h_i\)actual search direction: new gradient plus a memory of the old direction
\(\gamma_i\)how much memory to keep (Fletcher–Reeves ratio)
conjugate\(h_i^T A h_j = 0\): new steps don't re-disturb directions already minimized

🕹️ Try it: stretch the valley with the κ slider and drag the start. Count blue dots vs green dots.

Worked example: κ = 25 (click to open)

On \(f = \tfrac12(x^2 + 25y^2)\), steepest descent contracts the error by \(\frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1} = \frac{24}{26} = 0.923\) per step — 18 steps to reach \(10^{-6}\) from (10, 1). Conjugate gradient: error \(9.5 \to 2\times10^{-15}\) in 2 steps, because an n-dimensional quadratic has exactly n conjugate directions to spend. ✓ verified.

💡 Why robots care: least-squares costs (calibration, bundle adjustment, scan matching) are near-quadratic with huge, sparse A. CG never forms or factors A — it only needs products \(A h\) — which is why it (and its nonlinear cousins) powers large-scale SLAM back-ends.

7 · Stochastic search: when downhill isn't enough

Every method so far goes downhill, so every one gets trapped by the first local minimum (and for discrete spaces like tours there's no "downhill" at all). The fix is principled randomness, borrowed from physics. A system at temperature T occupies states by the Boltzmann distribution \(P(E) = e^{-E/kT}\); Metropolis (1953) turned it into an algorithm:

$$\text{accept a move with } \Delta E > 0 \text{ with probability } e^{-\Delta E / T} \qquad (\text{always accept } \Delta E \le 0)$$

Simulated annealing = Metropolis + a cooling schedule. The slides' four ingredients: (1) a state description, (2) a random perturbation move, (3) an objective E, (4) a temperature lowered over time. Hot → bold exploration; cold → greedy refinement; frozen → done.

🕹️ Try it: the slides' travelling-salesman example, live. Run anneal, then quench on the same cities — greedy freezes worse (3.953 vs 3.706; true optimum 3.634).

⚠️ Gotcha: annealing guarantees nothing on any finite schedule. It's the method of last resort for huge, lumpy, gradient-free spaces (TSP is NP-hard; chip layout; task ordering) — not a replacement for CG on smooth costs. Cooling too fast = quenching; you can watch that failure in the demo.

8 · Dynamic programming: optimal by construction

When the problem is a staged graph — finitely many states, finitely many actions per state — stop searching and enumerate smartly. Bellman's principle: any tail of an optimal path is itself optimal. So sweep backward from the goal, labeling each node with its cost-to-go:

$$V(n) = \min_{\text{edges } n \to m} \big[\, c(n, m) + V(m) \,\big], \qquad V(\text{goal}) = 0$$

then the optimal route is read off by walking downhill in V. That's Dijkstra, Viterbi, and grid path planning in one picture:

💡 Why robots care: a grid "cost map + backtrack" is exactly the slide's recipe for navigation — and the same backward-sweep idea, with expectations instead of minima, becomes value iteration in reinforcement learning. DP's price is the state space: it is exact and global, but only as big as you can enumerate.

9 · Summary

  • Extremum bracketing needs 3 points; golden section (0.618 per evaluation) is the bisection of minimization, and parabolic interpolation is its Brent-style accelerator.
  • No gradients? Simplex crawls anywhere, slowly. Smooth near-quadratic cost? Powell/CG exploit conjugacy — CG finishes an n-D quadratic in n steps.
  • Lumpy or discrete? Annealing buys global reach with randomness and patience.
  • Staged and enumerable? DP is exact: cost-to-go + backtrack.
  • Everything deterministic here is local — know which villain (local pits vs narrow valleys) your problem has before picking a weapon.

🧠 Self-check questions
1 · Why does bracketing a minimum need 3 points when a root needs 2?
A root is a sign change — two values of opposite sign certify it. A minimum is certified by a dip: you need a middle point lower than both ends. No 2 points can ever certify a dip.
2 · Where does the golden ratio come from in golden section?
Two demands: both outcomes shrink the bracket equally, and the new bracket has the same internal proportions (so the rule can repeat). Those give w² − 3w + 1 = 0, i.e. w = 0.382, shrink factor 0.618.
3 · Nelder–Mead never computes a derivative. What does it use instead, and what does that cost?
Only order comparisons of f at N+1 points — which is why it survives noisy, kinked, black-box costs. The cost: linear-ish convergence and painful crawling along curved valleys (watch the banana preset).
4 · Why exactly n steps for CG on an n-dimensional quadratic?
It builds directions that are A-conjugate: minimizing along a new one never un-minimizes the previous ones. n independent conjugate directions span the space, so after n exact line searches nothing is left. (Floating point blurs this slightly in practice.)
5 · In annealing, why ever accept a move that makes things worse?
Because the only path out of a local basin goes uphill. The Boltzmann rule accepts small uphill moves often and large ones rarely, scaled by T — hot enough to escape early, cold enough to converge late. At T = 0 you recover greedy descent and its traps (the quench button).
6 · Your path-planning grid has 10⁶ cells. Is DP feasible? What if the state includes velocity too?
10⁶ cells × a few actions is trivially feasible (one backward sweep). Add a 100-level velocity dimension and it's 10⁸ — borderline. State-space size is DP's only real enemy (the "curse of dimensionality").
