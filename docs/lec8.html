Lecture 8: Subspace Methods

The one-sentence version: an image is a point in million-dimensional space, but the images you actually meet live on a thin sliver of it — find that sliver (PCA), tilt it toward your labels (LDA), pair it with another signal (CCA), or split it into independent causes (ICA).

The big picture

This lecture is Lecture 2's eigen-decomposition finally paying off at scale. Every method here is "pick the eigenvectors of the right matrix":

reconstruct best → PCA (eigs of the covariance) · separate classes → LDA (eigs of \(S_W^{-1}S_B\)) · correlate two views → CCA (SVD of whitened cross-covariance) · independent causes → ICA (beyond 2nd order)

1 · Why: recognition in clutter

"Where's the can of Coke? Is the stove off?" A fridge shelf is clutter: segmentation is hard, pose and expression vary, illumination changes everything (the slides show one face under 21 lightings), and context fools even humans — the upside-down Thatcher face looks fine until you rotate it. The slides' answer: skip hand-built geometric models (generalized cylinders, CAD) and learn appearance directly from images — shape, reflectance, pose, and illumination all baked into training views, with well-defined error characteristics.

2 · The subspace idea

Stack an image's pixels into a vector: a 128×128 image is a point in \(\mathbb{R}^{16384}\). Random points in that space are static noise — real images of your objects occupy a tiny manifold. So approximate every training image as a mix of a few basis images:

$$x_i \approx \tilde x_i = \sum_{j=0}^{k} q_j(x_i)\,u_j, \qquad U = [u_0 \dots u_k],\; k \ll n$$
symbolmeaning
\(x_i\)one (normalized, zero-mean) training image as a long vector
\(u_j\)basis images ("eigenimages") — same size as x, orthonormal
\(q_j(x_i)\)the few numbers that now are the image: its coordinates in the subspace

Matching becomes cheap: comparing two images by \(\lVert x - y\rVert^2\) (or normalized correlation \(\rho = x^Ty / \lVert x\rVert\lVert y\rVert \ge \Theta\)) reduces to comparing their short coefficient vectors, \(\lVert q(x) - q(y) \rVert^2\) — distances survive the projection.

3 · PCA: the best possible basis

Which basis loses least? Minimize total reconstruction error \(\sum_i \lVert x_i - \sum_j q_j u_j \rVert^2\). The answer (Karhunen–Loève / PCA): take the top-k eigenvectors of the covariance \(C = XX^T\):

$$C\,u_i = \lambda_i u_i, \qquad q_i = U^T(x_i - \mu), \qquad x_i = U q_i + \mu$$

and the quality is exactly accountable:

$$\text{MSE}(m \text{ components}) = \sum_{j=m+1}^{N} \lambda_j \quad\text{— the eigenvalues you dropped}$$

🕹️ Try it: drag points and watch the axes, λ's and residuals react. The readout verifies MSE = dropped λ live. The two clusters preset shows PCA's blind spot.

💡 Three readings of the same fact (slides): PCA minimizes reconstruction error, maximizes projected variance, and finds the data's "natural" coordinate system. One eigendecomposition, three interpretations.

4 · The small-matrix trick (how eigenfaces are computable at all)

For images, \(C = XX^T\) is m×m with m = #pixels — e.g. 16384×16384. But with only n images (n ≪ m), use the n×n inner-product matrix instead:

$$Q = X^TX \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}, \qquad Q v_i' = \lambda_i' v_i' \;\Rightarrow\; u_i = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_i'}}\,X v_i'$$

Why it works: multiply \(X^TXv = \lambda v\) by X on the left and \(XX^T(Xv) = \lambda (Xv)\) — every eigenvector of Q lifts to one of C. Verified numerically: a 500×500 eigenproblem solved via an 8×8 one, eigenvector agreement |cos| = 1.0000000000.

5 · Recognition and pose from coefficients

Training views taken around an object (the slides' porcelain cat on a turntable) project to a smooth curve in eigenspace — a parametric manifold indexed by pose. At runtime: project the new image (\(a_i = \langle x, e_i\rangle\), inner products = a least-squares fit), find the nearest point on the manifold, and that point names both the object and its pose. The same machinery localizes mobile robots from panoramas.

⚠️ Fine print (slides): the coefficients need the complete image — occlusion in clutter breaks the plain inner product. That's exactly what the lecture's reference paper (robust eigenimages) addresses, by estimating coefficients from subsets of pixels.

6 · LDA: separation, not reconstruction

PCA is unsupervised — it never sees labels, so its best-variance axis can be the worst axis for telling classes apart. Fisher's linear discriminant optimizes what you actually want:

$$\rho(W) = \frac{W^T S_B W}{W^T S_W W} \qquad\to\qquad S_B W = \lambda\, S_W W$$
symbolmeaning
\(S_W = \sum_i \sum_{x\in\chi_i}(x-\mu_i)(x-\mu_i)^T\)within-class scatter: how fat each class blob is
\(S_B = \sum_i |\chi_i|(\mu_i - \mu)(\mu_i - \mu)^T\)between-class scatter: how far apart the class means sit
\(W\)projection direction(s) — at most c−1 of them for c classes

🕹️ Try it: drag the class centers; PCA ignores you, LDA tracks the separation. Watch the two 1-D error readouts.

💡 Fisherfaces (Belhumeur et al., 1997): for images \(S_W\) is always singular (more pixels than training images), so you can't invert it. The fix is a cascade: PCA first down to n−c dimensions, then LDA down to c−1: \(W_{opt} = W_{LDA}^T\,U_{PCA}^T\). On the classic benchmark, Fisherfaces beat eigenfaces soundly (the slides' chart shows ~7% vs ~24% error close-cropped) — though LDA is noise-sensitive, and the slides note modern large-scale systems score ~97%+.

7 · CCA: correlating two sets of measurements

Supervised in a different way: given paired observations x (e.g. images) and y (e.g. pose angles), find directions \(w_x, w_y\) whose projections correlate maximally:

$$\rho = \frac{w_x^T C_{xy} w_y}{\sqrt{w_x^T C_{xx} w_x\; w_y^T C_{yy} w_y}} \qquad\to\qquad A w = \mu B w \;\;\text{(generalized eigenproblem)}$$

computed stably via SVD of the whitened cross-covariance: \(A = C_{xx}^{-1/2} C_{xy} C_{yy}^{-1/2} = UDV^T\), \(w_{xi} = C_{xx}^{-1/2}u_i\), \(w_{yi} = C_{yy}^{-1/2}v_i\). Properties: at most min(p, q, n) factors, affine-invariant, factors orthogonal in the covariance metric. Verified on a toy pair of 2-D signals sharing one latent variable: canonical correlations (0.93, 0.04) — it finds the shared channel and ignores the rest.

Robotics use (slides): regression tasks like pose estimation — correlate the image eigenspace with the pose parameters directly.

8 · ICA: independence, not just decorrelation

PCA uses only 2nd-order statistics (covariance). ICA assumes the data are a linear mixture of statistically independent, non-Gaussian sources — the cocktail-party problem:

$$X = AS \qquad \text{given only } X,\text{ estimate both } A \text{ and } S$$

The standard recipe: whiten the data (PCA + rescaling), then rotate to maximize non-Gaussianity (e.g. |kurtosis|) — because by the central limit theorem, mixtures are more Gaussian than their ingredients. Caveats from the slides: needs non-Gaussian sources, gives no ordering, components aren't orthogonal.

🕹️ Try it: the sensors see only the orange mixtures. Slide θ — every rotation looks the same to variance, but kurtosis peaks exactly at the unmixing angle.

The slides' gallery: ICA on natural image patches yields localized edge filters (sparse coding — strikingly like V1 receptive fields), noise suppression, and ICA "faces" that are local features (eyes, mouths) where PCA's are global ghost-faces.

9 · Summary

  • Images are points; real image sets are thin manifolds. Subspace methods find the manifold and work there.
  • PCA: eigenvectors of the covariance; error = dropped eigenvalues; computable for images via the n×n inner-product trick.
  • Recognition = nearest point in coefficient space; pose = position along the training manifold.
  • The task picks the matrix: reconstruction → PCA, separation → LDA (PCA-then-LDA for images), paired regression → CCA, source separation → ICA.
  • These are the building blocks the slides promised back in Lecture 2: eigen-decomposition as insight, now at 16384 dimensions.

🧠 Self-check questions
1 · Your training set is 100 images of 256×256. What size eigenproblem do you actually solve, and why?
100×100 (XᵀX), not 65536×65536 (XXᵀ). Each eigenvector v of XᵀX lifts to an eigenimage u = Xv/√λ with the same eigenvalue. n images can span at most n directions, so nothing is lost.
2 · You keep components until 95% of the summed eigenvalues. What exactly did you just guarantee?
That the average reconstruction MSE is 5% of the total variance — because MSE(k) = Σ of dropped λ. It guarantees nothing about classification: a tiny-λ direction can carry all the class information (see the LDA demo).
3 · Why is S_W always singular for image data, and what's the standard fix?
rank(S_W) ≤ n − c (images minus classes), far below the pixel count, so it can't be inverted. Fisherfaces: project to n−c dims with PCA first, then run LDA there.
4 · After whitening, why can't 2nd-order statistics finish the ICA job?
Whitened data has identity covariance, and every rotation of it still does — covariance is rotation-blind. Only higher-order statistics (kurtosis, negentropy) change with the rotation and can point at the independent axes.
5 · ICA on two Gaussian sources: what happens?
Nothing useful — a rotated pair of Gaussians is still an identical pair of Gaussians, so no rotation is "more independent" than another. ICA requires non-Gaussian sources (the slides' warning), and the kurtosis curve in the demo would be flat.
6 · When would you pick CCA over plain PCA-plus-regression for pose estimation?
When the image variance is dominated by nuisance factors (lighting, background) uncorrelated with pose: PCA spends components on them, while CCA finds image directions that specifically co-vary with the pose signal.
