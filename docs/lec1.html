2 · Positions and reference frames

A position is always of something, with respect to something:

$${}^j p_i = \begin{pmatrix} {}^j p_{x_i} \\ {}^j p_{y_i} \\ {}^j p_{z_i} \end{pmatrix} \quad\text{— the position of } i \text{ expressed in frame } j$$

The leading superscript is the frame doing the measuring; the subscript is the thing measured. Robots are forests of frames — the slides' AIBO dog has one per joint — and bugs in frame bookkeeping are the classic robotics bug. Read \({}^k R_j\,{}^j R_i\) like plumbing: the inner j's must match, and they "cancel", leaving \({}^k R_i\).

3 · Rotation matrices

The rotation between frames i and j is the matrix of all basis-vector dot products (\({}^j R_i\) with entries like \(\vec x_i \cdot \vec x_j\)) — i.e. each column is one of frame i's axes expressed in frame j. The three elementary rotations:

$$R_z(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} c_\theta & -s_\theta & 0\\ s_\theta & c_\theta & 0\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad R_y(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} c_\theta & 0 & s_\theta\\ 0&1&0\\ -s_\theta & 0 & c_\theta\end{pmatrix},\quad R_x(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} 1&0&0\\ 0&c_\theta & -s_\theta\\ 0&s_\theta & c_\theta\end{pmatrix}$$
propertymeaning
columns orthonormal, det = +1rotations preserve lengths and handedness — a free sanity check for your code
\(R^{-1} = R^T\)inverting a rotation is free
9 numbers, 3 DOFredundant but singularity-free — the "true" representation the others approximate
⚠️ Gotcha: the odd-one-out sign in \(R_y\) (the −s on the bottom left) is not a typo — it's what keeps the cyclic pattern x→y→z→x consistent. Memorize the pattern, not the matrices.

4 · Composing rotations: order matters

$${}^k R_i = {}^k R_j\,{}^j R_i, \qquad\text{but}\qquad {}^k R_j\,{}^j R_i \ne {}^j R_i\,{}^k R_j$$

Matrix products apply right-to-left, and in 3-D they do not commute.

🕹️ Try it: the same two 90° rotations in both orders. The planes end up in genuinely different poses (max matrix difference 1.0 — verified).

5 · Euler angles: three readable numbers, one trap

The slides' convention is ZYX: \(R = R_z(\alpha)R_y(\beta)R_x(\gamma)\) — yaw, then pitch about the new y, then roll about the newest x. Extraction from a rotation matrix:

$$\beta = \mathrm{atan2}\!\left(-r_{31}, \sqrt{r_{11}^2 + r_{21}^2}\right),\quad \alpha = \mathrm{atan2}\!\left(\tfrac{r_{21}}{\cos\beta}, \tfrac{r_{11}}{\cos\beta}\right),\quad \gamma = \mathrm{atan2}\!\left(\tfrac{r_{32}}{\cos\beta}, \tfrac{r_{33}}{\cos\beta}\right)$$

Round trip verified: (0.4, −0.7, 1.1) → R → (0.4, −0.7, 1.1) ✓. Note the cos β divisions: the formulas die exactly where the representation does.

The trap: at β = ±90° the roll axis lines up with the yaw axis — two of your three numbers then do the same thing, and one degree of freedom vanishes from the chart (the rotation itself is fine). Verified to machine precision: \(R(\alpha{=}0.3, 90°, \gamma{=}0.5) = R(0.8, 90°, 1.0)\) exactly. Same disease as latitude/longitude at the poles (the slides' globe).

🕹️ Try it: hit the gimbal-lock preset, then "wiggle α and γ together" — both sliders sweep while the airplane holds perfectly still.

6 · Quaternions: the singularity-free fix

No 3-parameter representation can avoid singularities (proved); a 4-parameter one can. Hamilton's quaternions (1843): \(\vec\epsilon = \epsilon_0 + \epsilon_1\vec i + \epsilon_2\vec j + \epsilon_3\vec k\) with \(\vec i\vec i = \vec j\vec j = \vec k\vec k = -1\), \(\vec i\vec j = \vec k\) (and anti-cyclic products negative). A rotation by θ about unit axis \(\vec a\):

$$\vec\epsilon = \Big(\cos\tfrac\theta2,\; \vec a \sin\tfrac\theta2\Big)$$
operationhow
compose rotationsquaternion product (the slides' 16-term formula) — verified: q_a q_b ↔ R_a R_b
quaternion → matrixthe slides' \(1 - 2(\epsilon_2^2+\epsilon_3^2)\)… formula — verified against R_z(90°)
matrix → quaternion\(\epsilon_0 = \tfrac12\sqrt{1 + r_{11} + r_{22} + r_{33}}\), then \(\epsilon_1 = \tfrac{r_{32}-r_{23}}{4\epsilon_0}\), … — round trip verified
inversenegate the vector part (unit quaternions)
💡 Why bother: 4 numbers, one constraint (unit norm), no gimbal lock, numerically stable composition, and clean interpolation (slerp) — which is why graphics, vision, and every flight controller use them. As the slides say twice: the good news is there are standard libraries. Know what they fix; don't hand-roll them.

7 · Homogeneous transforms & joints

Rotation plus translation, \({}^j\vec p = {}^jR_i\,{}^i\vec p + \vec t\), becomes a single matrix product by appending a 1 to every point:

$$\begin{pmatrix} {}^j p \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} {}^jR_i & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} {}^i p \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad {}^jP = {}^jT_i\,{}^iP$$

Now chains of frames are chains of matrix products. Standard joints are just parameterized T's: revolute (R_z(θ), no translation), prismatic (identity rotation, translation d along an axis), cylindrical (R_θ plus d along z), spherical (full 3-DOF rotation) — and most mechanisms, like the slides' 6-axis KUKA KR15, are products of these.

Worked example: rotate-then-translate (click to open)

T with R = R_z(90°), t = (1, 2, 0). Then (1, 0, 0) → R·p + t = (0, 1, 0) + (1, 2, 0) = (1, 3, 0) ✓. And the inverse is not "negate t": \(T^{-1} = \big[\,R^T \;\; -R^T t\,\big]\) (verified T⁻¹T = I). Translation happens in the rotated frame — a classic bug source.

Closing teaser (slides): velocities will come later — forward kinematics \(v = \Phi\dot q\), inverse \(\dot q = \Phi^{-1}v\). That Φ is Lecture 2's matrix A: the whole linear-systems lecture is secretly about this equation.

8 · Summary

  • Every quantity carries a frame; transforms move quantities between frames, and superscript/subscript plumbing keeps you honest.
  • Rotation matrices: orthonormal, det +1, inverse = transpose, singularity-free, composable — but 9 numbers and order-sensitive.
  • Euler angles: 3 readable numbers, ZYX convention here, extraction via atan2 — and a genuine DOF loss at β = ±90°.
  • Quaternions: (cos θ/2, a sin θ/2); slightly alien algebra, zero singularities; use a library.
  • Homogeneous 4×4s unify R and t; joints are parameterized transforms; robots are products of them.

🧠 Self-check questions
1 · Your code produces a "rotation" matrix with det = −1. What happened?
A reflection snuck in (often from swapping two axes or a left-handed frame). Lengths are preserved but handedness flips — downstream cross-products will silently point the wrong way.
2 · Why can't any 3-number attitude representation be singularity-free?
Topology: the rotation group SO(3) can't be covered by a single 3-D chart without degenerate points (same reason a globe can't have a flat map without poles misbehaving). Four numbers with a constraint — quaternions — sidestep it.
3 · At gimbal lock, is the aircraft's rotation itself degenerate?
No — the rotation matrix is perfectly healthy. Only the Euler-angle chart degenerates: α and γ become redundant (only α − γ matters at β = +90°), so rates about one world axis become unrepresentable as finite angle-rates.
4 · {}^kR_j and {}^jR_i are known. Express frame k's view of a point measured in frame i, and say why the other product order is meaningless.
{}^k p = {}^kR_j {}^jR_i {}^i p. The inner frame indices must match (j with j); the reversed product pairs mismatched frames — the "units" of the plumbing don't cancel.
5 · T = [R t; 0 1]. Why is T⁻¹ = [Rᵀ −Rᵀt; 0 1] and not [Rᵀ −t; 0 1]?
Undoing "rotate then translate" requires translating back in the already-unrotated frame: p = Rp′ + t ⇒ p′ = Rᵀp − Rᵀt. Verified numerically on the worked example.
