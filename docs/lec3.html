Lecture 3: Interpolation

The one-sentence version: interpolation fills the gaps between your samples with a function you can evaluate anywhere — and the craft is choosing how much that function is allowed to bend: straight lines, one big polynomial (dangerous), stitched cubics (the workhorse), or a stochastic model that also tells you how unsure it is.

1 · Model-based vs data-driven

Two regimes (slides): model-based — domain knowledge supplies the function family (battery recharge curves, drive-train dynamics, grasp material models); data-driven — all you have is samples and maybe constraints (driving in traffic, painting). This lecture is the data-driven toolkit. The theoretical safety net is the Weierstrass approximation theorem: every continuous f on [a, b] can be approximated arbitrarily well by some polynomial. Wonderful — except it names no strategy for finding it, and the obvious strategy goes wrong (Section 3).

2 · Linear interpolation: the baseline

$$p(x) = f(x_i) + \frac{f(x_{i+1}) - f(x_i)}{x_{i+1} - x_i}\,(x - x_i), \qquad x \in [x_i, x_{i+1}]$$

Equivalently \(y = Ay_j + By_{j+1}\) with \(A = \frac{x_{j+1}-x}{x_{j+1}-x_j}\), \(B = 1-A\) — weights that fade linearly from one neighbor to the other (a 2-point special case of Lagrange). Continuous, local, monotone, cheap; kinked at every sample, and it cuts every corner (HW2 measures exactly how much).

3 · Lagrange: one polynomial through everything

n+1 points determine a unique degree-n polynomial. The direct route — solve \(V a = y\) for coefficients of \(a_0 + a_1x + \dots\) — builds the Vandermonde matrix, and it is numerically rotten:

Lagrange's form writes the same polynomial without ever solving a system:

$$p(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n y_k L_k(x), \qquad L_k(x) = \prod_{i \ne k} \frac{x - x_i}{x_k - x_i}, \qquad L_k(x_i) = \delta_{ik}$$
symbolmeaning
\(L_k\)a "switch" polynomial: 1 at its own point, 0 at every other
\(y_k L_k\)each data point contributes itself, switched on only near home
Worked example: the slides' (x−1)² recovery (click to close)

Points (0, 1), (1, 0): p(x) = 1·L₀ = \(\frac{x-1}{0-1}\) = −x + 1 — a line, exact at both points. Add (−1, 4): now L₀ = −(x−1)(x+1), L₁ = "don't care" (its y is 0), L₂ = ½x(x−1), and

$$p(x) = -(x-1)(x+1) + 4\cdot\tfrac12 x(x-1) = (x-1)(-x-1+2x) = (x-1)^2 \;✓$$

Three samples of a parabola reproduce the parabola exactly — interpolation is exact when the truth is in the family. Verified.

⚠️ The slides' warning, quantified: "meandering between data points can become significant." One global polynomial must wiggle through everything — with many or uneven points the swings grow (Runge's phenomenon, revisited properly in Lecture 4).

🕹️ Try it: add points and watch the red polynomial start to swing while the green spline stays calm. "Load Runge example" is the classic failure.

4 · Cubic splines: stitched, not stretched

Why cubics? Demand the 4th derivative be zero on each piece and you get the lowest-degree polynomial that can still match value and curvature at both ends: \(f = \frac{c_1}{6}x^3 + \frac{c_2}{2}x^2 + c_3x + c_4\) per interval. Numerical Recipes' form extends linear interpolation with a curvature correction:

$$y = Ay_j + By_{j+1} + Cy''_j + Dy''_{j+1}, \qquad C = \tfrac16(A^3 - A)h^2, \quad D = \tfrac16(B^3 - B)h^2$$

The \(y''_j\) are chosen so slopes match across every knot — a tridiagonal system (each knot only talks to its neighbors), solved in O(n). Verified against scipy to 10⁻¹⁶.

💡 Why robots love splines: curvature ≈ acceleration. A spline joint trajectory has continuous velocity and acceleration — no actuator spikes at the via-points — and each sample only influences its neighborhood, unlike Lagrange where one bad sample pollutes the whole curve.

5 · Multi-variate: bilinear interpolation

On a grid (images, lidar heightmaps, cost maps), interpolate inside a cell from its 4 corners:

$$y(x_1, x_2) = (1-t)(1-u)\,y_0 + t(1-u)\,y_1 + tu\,y_2 + (1-t)u\,y_3$$

with t, u the fractional positions in the cell. The weights are the areas of the opposite rectangles, they always sum to 1, and the formula is exact for planes (verified).

🕹️ Try it: drag the ★; each corner's weight is the area of the rectangle diagonally opposite it.

6 · Kriging: interpolation that knows its own uncertainty

Treat the data as samples of a stochastic process and estimate the most likely function — "Gaussian process regression" (the slides' spelling "Kringing" is a typo that stuck). Model f as a weighted sum of basis functions:

$$f(x) = \sum_i w_i\,\phi_i(x) = \vec w\,\Phi(\vec x) \qquad \phi_i:\; \text{polynomial } x^i \;\big/\; \text{Gaussian } e^{-(x-x_i)^2/2s} \;\big/\; \text{sigmoid}$$

Maximum likelihood with Gaussian noise ⟹ least squares (Lecture 2 again):

$$w_{ML} = (\Phi^T\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^T\vec y \qquad\xrightarrow{\;\text{regularize}\;}\qquad w = (\lambda I + \Phi^T\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^T\vec y$$
knobwhat it does
s (bump width)how far each measurement's influence reaches — small s: wiggly and unsure between points
λ (regularization / noise)how much to trust each point — λ > 0 stops exact interpolation and starts smoothing (verified: λ = 0.3 leaves 0.18 residuals at the data)
the variance bandthe model's own confidence — tight at data, wide in gaps (the slides' California maps: prediction map + variance map)

🕹️ Try it: add and drag measurements; shrink s to see uncertainty balloon between points; raise λ to watch the curve stop passing through the dots.

💡 Why robots care: the variance band is a plan: "measure next where I'm most unsure" — the heart of informative-path-planning and of the fire-monitoring coverage example from Lecture 6. Interpolators that only give a value can't say that.

7 · Summary

  • Interpolation = exact at samples; approximation = close everywhere (Lecture 4's subject). Weierstrass promises a good polynomial exists; it doesn't say the interpolating one is it.
  • Never solve the Vandermonde system; use the Lagrange/barycentric form — and fear global polynomials on many points.
  • Cubic splines: linear interpolation + curvature correction, tridiagonal O(n) solve, local and smooth — the default.
  • Bilinear: 4 corners, opposite-area weights, exact for planes.
  • Kriging/GP: ML fitting of basis functions; λ trades interpolation for smoothing; you get error bars for free.

⚠️ Typos in the slides to watch out for
  • Throughout: "Kringing" → Kriging (after D. G. Krige).
  • Slide 11: in \(L_k(x) = \prod\) the condition reads "i = 0, i ≠ j" → i ≠ k; and "y_kL_k(x_k) = y_kL_k(x)" should read p(x_k) = y_k (the δ_ik line below it is the real statement).
  • Slide 14: "L₁ = Don't care" is correct but only because a₁ = 0 — the slide's a-values are the y-values (a₀=1, a₁=0, a₂=4), a notation collision with the polynomial coefficients a_i from slide 9.
  • Slide 32: regularized solution "(λ + ΦᵀΦ)⁻¹" → (λI + ΦᵀΦ)⁻¹.
  • Slide 19: C and D carry (x_{j+1} − x_j)² — the h² of the current interval; easy to mis-code with non-uniform knots.
🧠 Self-check questions
1 · Interpolation vs approximation — and which does HW2's Q1 ask for?
Interpolation passes exactly through the samples (what HW2 wants between kept waypoints); approximation minimizes error overall without hitting points (Lecture 4, and HW2-Q2's table bounds). Noisy data usually deserves approximation — hitting noise exactly is memorizing it.
2 · Why is solving V·a = y bad but evaluating the Lagrange form fine, if they're the same polynomial?
The polynomial is the same; the arithmetic isn't. V's columns (1, x, x², …) become nearly parallel — condition 10¹⁶ by n = 20 — so the coefficient solve amplifies rounding catastrophically. Lagrange/barycentric evaluates without ever forming that system.
3 · Why does each spline knot only need to "talk" to its neighbors, and what does that buy?
Continuity of slope at knot j involves only y″ at j−1, j, j+1 — hence a tridiagonal system, O(n) to solve, and locality: moving one point only reshapes nearby pieces (drag a point in the demo and watch).
4 · Your bilinear-sampled cost map shows diamond-shaped artifacts along cell diagonals. Why?
Bilinear is linear along rows and columns but quadratic (tu term) along diagonals, and its gradient jumps at cell edges. The artifacts are those derivative kinks; bicubic interpolation (the 2-D spline analogue) smooths them.
5 · In the kriging demo, what happens to the variance band at an isolated data point as λ grows, and why is that honest?
The band no longer pinches to zero at the point — λ says "measurements are noisy", so even at a sample the model keeps residual uncertainty. Exact interpolation of noisy data would be false confidence.
