The big picture

Interpolation (Lecture 3) answers "what's between my samples?". Approximation answers a different question: I can evaluate f anywhere — give me a cheaper stand-in I can trust everywhere. You choose where to sample, and it turns out where is most of the game. The lecture's arc: define the right error measure (the worst case, not the average) → characterize the optimal polynomial (equioscillation) → meet the family that achieves it almost automatically (Chebyshev) → use it to shrink lookup tables and truncate series.

1 · Why approximate instead of interpolate

Interpolation is a hard constraint: hit every sample exactly. But the samples were just the points you happened to have — and Lecture 3 showed that forcing a degree-20 polynomial through evenly spaced samples makes it worse between them (Runge). Approximation relaxes the constraint: never mind hitting samples, minimize the distance to f itself. The surprise of this lecture is that the two stories reconnect at the end: the best practical approximation is an interpolation — through the right points.

2 · The L∞ yardstick

Distance between f and an approximation p over an interval:

$$\lVert f - p\rVert_\infty \;=\; \max_{a\le x\le b}\,\lvert f(x)-p(x)\rvert, \qquad d(f,\pi_n) \;=\; \min_{p\,\in\,\pi_n}\, \lVert f - p\rVert_\infty$$
symbolmeaning
\(\lVert f-p\rVert_\infty\)the worst miss anywhere in the interval (not the average miss — that's least squares, Lecture 2)
\(\pi_n\)all polynomials of degree ≤ n
\(d(f,\pi_n)\)how well degree n can possibly do — the minimax distance

Weierstrass guarantees \(d(f,\pi_n)\to 0\) for any continuous f: polynomials can come arbitrarily close, if you allow the degree to grow. The question is how fast.

3 · The equioscillation theorem

How do you recognize the best polynomial when you see it? Chebyshev's characterization:

$$p^{*} \text{ is the best in } \pi_n \iff f - p^{*} \text{ attains } \pm\lVert f-p^{*}\rVert_\infty \text{ at } \ge n+2 \text{ points, alternating in sign}$$

Intuition: if the error had one lonely worst peak, you could tilt p slightly to shave that peak without creating a bigger one elsewhere — so at the optimum the error must be pinned at both ends of its range, +max, −max, +max, … so many times that no degree-n adjustment can push all the peaks down at once (a degree-n polynomial can't out-wiggle n+2 alternations).

Key idea: "best worst-case" has a visual signature: a ripple of equal height. If your error plot has one big bump, you're not done; if it looks like an even wave touching ±ε, you can stop — nobody can do better.

4 · Worked out fully: the best line for eˣ

The slides' running example: approximate \(f(x)=e^x\) on \([-1,1]\) by a line \(p(x)=a+bx\) (n = 1, so the theorem demands 3 alternating extreme points). The error \(e(x)=e^x-a-bx\) is convex, so its extremes are the two endpoints plus one interior minimum — exactly three, forced automatically.

Slope. Alternation with equal values at both endpoints means \(e(-1)=e(1)\):

$$e^{-1}-(a-b) = e^{1}-(a+b)\;\Rightarrow\; b=\frac{e-e^{-1}}{2}=1.1752$$

The minimax slope is the average slope of f across the interval — the line "keeps up" with the function overall.

Interior point. The interior extreme is where the error is flat: \(e'(x_1) = e^{x_1}-b = 0 \Rightarrow x_1=\ln b = 0.16144\).

Height. Alternation between x = −1 and x₁ means \(e(-1)=-e(x_1)\), which solves to

$$a=\frac{e - b\,x_1}{2} = 1.2643, \qquad \lVert f-p^{*}\rVert_\infty = 0.2788 \;\text{ at } x=-1,\;0.16144,\;+1 \;(+,-,+)$$

Three touches, alternating — the certificate. A Nelder–Mead search over all (a, b) lands on the same line to 5 decimals (scripts/lec4/verify.py).

5 · Divided differences & the lower bound

The theorem certifies an answer once you have it. The slides also derive a floor — a number no line can beat — using Lecture 3's divided differences:

$$f[x_0,\dots,x_k] = \frac{f[x_1,\dots,x_k]-f[x_0,\dots,x_{k-1}]}{x_k-x_0}, \qquad f[x_0,\dots,x_k] = \sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{f(x_i)}{w'(x_i)},\;\; w=\textstyle\prod(x-x_i)$$

A degree-1 polynomial has zero second divided difference, so for any line p, on any 3 nodes, \(f[x_0,x_1,x_2] = (f-p)[x_0,x_1,x_2]\), and bounding each term gives

$$d(f,\pi_1)\;\ge\;\frac{\lvert f[x_0,x_1,x_2]\rvert}{\sum_i 1/\lvert w'(x_i)\rvert} \;\overset{x=(-1,0,1)}{=}\;\frac{f(-1)-2f(0)+f(1)}{4} = \frac{e - 2 + e^{-1}}{4} = 0.2715$$

Sanity check: 0.2715 ≤ 0.2788 ✓ — the floor sits just 2.6% under the true optimum. Three strategically chosen points already know almost everything about how hard the problem is.

6 · Chebyshev polynomials

The family that makes minimax practical — a cosine wearing a polynomial costume:

$$T_n(x) = \cos\!\big(n\arccos x\big), \qquad T_{n+1}(x) = 2x\,T_n(x) - T_{n-1}(x), \quad T_0 = 1,\; T_1 = x$$
propertystatementwhy it matters
bounded\(\lvert T_n(x)\rvert \le 1\) on [−1,1]equal ripple built in — every wiggle has the same height
zeros\(x_k = \cos\frac{\pi(k+\tfrac12)}{N}\), k = 0…N−1the Chebyshev points — where you should sample
extrema\(\cos\frac{\pi k}{N}\), values ±1 alternatingN+1 alternations: exactly the equioscillation pattern
orthogonal\(\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{T_i T_j}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}dx = 0\) (i ≠ j), and discretely over the zeroscoefficients come from sums, not a linear solve — no Vandermonde, no 10¹⁶ condition number
extremalleading coefficient \(2^{\,n-1}\); among monic degree-n polynomials, \(T_n/2^{n-1}\) has the smallest max on [−1,1]this is the minimax property, stated about polynomials themselves

The circle picture is the whole trick: in angle space \(\theta=\arccos x\), T_n is literally \(\cos n\theta\) — perfectly even ripples. Projecting the circle onto the line squeezes points toward the ends, and that extra end-density is precisely what cancels interpolation's end-of-interval blowup.

7 · Chebyshev approximation

To approximate f, sample it at the N zeros of \(T_N\) and compute coefficients by a plain sum (the discrete orthogonality doing the work of a matrix solve):

$$c_j = \frac{2}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} f(x_k)\,T_j(x_k), \qquad p(x) = \Big[\sum_{k=0}^{m-1} c_k T_k(x)\Big] - \tfrac12 c_0$$
symbolmeaning
\(x_k\)zeros of \(T_N\): \(\cos\frac{\pi(k+1/2)}{N}\)
\(c_j\)j-th Chebyshev coefficient; for smooth f they decay geometrically
\(m\le N\)how many terms you keep — truncate where the \(c_j\) drop below your error budget
\(-\tfrac12 c_0\)bookkeeping: the j = 0 sum double-counts, so half of \(c_0\) is subtracted

Truncating after m terms leaves an error ≈ the first dropped term, \(c_m T_m(x)\) — and \(T_m\) oscillates evenly between ±1, so the error is spread evenly: automatically very close to minimax. Not exactly equal to it: for the eˣ line, Chebyshev gives \(1.2661 + 1.1303x\) with worst error 0.3219 — 15% above the true minimax 0.2788, at essentially zero effort. That trade (give up a few percent, skip the Remez iteration entirely) is why everyone uses Chebyshev in practice.

8 · Any interval: domain warping

Everything above lives on [−1, 1]. For an arbitrary interval [a, b], change variables:

$$y = \frac{x - \tfrac12(b+a)}{\tfrac12(b-a)} \;\in\;[-1,1] \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad x = \tfrac12(b-a)\,y + \tfrac12(b+a)$$

Shift by the midpoint, scale by the half-width. Approximate \(g(y)=f(x(y))\) with Chebyshev machinery, then substitute back. Robotics version: your joint angle lives in [0, 2π], your cos table in [0, π/2] — warp first, fit second.

9 · Truncating power series

The slides close with the cheapest approximation of all: take a series you already know and cut it. For \(f(x) = \frac{1}{2+x}\) on [0, 1]:

$$\frac{1}{2+x} = \frac12 - \frac{x}{4} + \frac{x^2}{8} - \cdots = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n x^n}{2^{n+1}}$$

Each term is only half the previous one, so the cut-off error shrinks by 2 per term: reaching \(10^{-9}\) takes about 30 terms (the remainder bound \((1/2)^n\) crosses \(10^{-9}\) at n = 30; the measured error, helped by alternating cancellation, crosses at 29). Re-expand the same function in Chebyshev polynomials on the warped interval and the coefficients fall ~10× per term — the same \(10^{-9}\) now costs 9 terms. In your control loop that's a 3× cheaper evaluation for the identical guarantee.

Worked example: the slides' 6-step truncation recipe (click to open)
  1. Warp the interval to [−1, 1]: here x = (y+1)/2, so \(g(y) = \frac{1}{2+(y+1)/2} = \frac{2}{5+y}\).
  2. Pick N generously (say 60) and compute \(c_j\) at the \(T_N\) zeros by the sum formula.
  3. Look at the \(\lvert c_j\rvert\): 0.816, 0.083, 0.0083, 0.00084, … — dividing by ~9.9 each step.
  4. Keep terms until the first dropped one is under budget: \(\lvert c_9\rvert \approx 9\times10^{-10} < 10^{-9}\) → keep m = 9 terms, c₀…c₈.
  5. Evaluate p by the recurrence (never expand into powers of x — that re-introduces the ill-conditioning).
  6. Verify: measured max error with 9 terms = 1.0 × 10⁻⁹, with 8 terms = 9.9 × 10⁻⁹ (verify.py). ✓

10 · Summary

  • Approximation ≠ interpolation: minimize the worst error over the interval, not the error at fixed samples.
  • The optimum has a signature: the error equioscillates — n+2 touches of ±ε with alternating signs. One lonely peak means you can still improve.
  • Divided differences give certified lower bounds: for eˣ, no line beats 0.2715; the true best is 0.2788.
  • Chebyshev polynomials are cosines in disguise — equal ripple is their native state, and sampling at their zeros makes interpolation nearly minimax (0.3219 vs 0.2788 for the line: 15% worse, zero iteration).
  • Chebyshev-truncate your power series: same 10⁻⁹ promise, 9 terms instead of 30.

⚠️ Typos in the slides to watch out for
  • Equioscillation slide: "points at which the error is f(x) = p(x)" → should be f(x) − p(x) (the error attains ±max there, it isn't zero there — the zeros are between the extremes).
  • Domain-warping slide: the map is printed as \(y = \frac{x - \frac12(b-a)}{\frac12(b-a)}\) → the numerator should subtract the midpoint \(\frac12(b+a)\), not the half-width. As printed it sends [a,b] to [−0.2, 1.8] for a=2, b=7 instead of [−1, 1] (checked in verify.py).
  • The "30 terms vs 9 terms" example is stated for 1/(2+x) on [0, 1] (the warped fit) — on [−1, 1] itself the pole at −2 is closer and Chebyshev needs ~17 terms. The slide doesn't say the interval out loud; the numbers only work on [0, 1].
  • "Chebyshev approximation … for periodic functions": T-expansions work for any continuous f on a finite interval — the cosine connection is a change of variables, not a periodicity requirement.
🧠 Self-check questions
1 · Your error plot for a degree-3 fit shows peaks +ε, −ε, +ε, −ε, +ε of equal height. Are you done?
Yes. n = 3 needs n + 2 = 5 alternating touches — that's exactly what you have, so by the equioscillation theorem no degree-3 polynomial does better. The plot is the optimality certificate.
2 · Why is the minimax slope for eˣ the average slope (e − 1/e)/2 and not f′ somewhere?
Equal error at both endpoints forces e(−1) = e(1), i.e. f(1) − f(−1) = p(1) − p(−1) = 2b. The line must gain exactly as much as f does across the interval — its slope is the mean slope. (By the MVT it does equal f′ somewhere: at x₁ = ln b, which is exactly the interior ripple point.)
3 · Where does the ¼ in the lower bound (f(−1) − 2f(0) + f(1))/4 come from?
Nodes −1, 0, 1 give |w′| = 2, 1, 2, so Σ1/|w′| = ½ + 1 + ½ = 2; and the second divided difference is (f(−1) − 2f(0) + f(1))/2. Divide the two: the extra factor 2·2 = 4.
4 · Your lookup table is sampled at evenly spaced angles. A teammate says "just use more points". What's the Chebyshev counter-offer?
Keep the same number of points but place them at cos(π(k+½)/N) (scaled to your interval): the error stops piling up at the ends and drops to near-minimax. More evenly spaced points on a Runge-like response can make things worse; better-placed points never do.
5 · Chebyshev coefficients of a smooth f drop ~10× per term. Terms cost 1 µs each and you have a 10⁻⁶ error budget. How many terms?
You need 10× decay to run ~6 orders from c₀ ≈ O(1): about 6–7 terms, ≈ 7 µs. The decay rate is the design parameter — it's set by how close f's nearest singularity sits to your interval, which is why warping to a smaller interval (pushing the pole relatively farther) made 1/(2+x) so cheap.
6 · Why evaluate the kept terms with the recurrence instead of expanding p(x) into 1, x, x², …?
Expanding into monomials reinstates the huge, cancelling coefficients that made Vandermonde ill-conditioned in Lecture 3 (T₁₀'s leading coefficient is 2⁹ = 512). The recurrence Tn+1 = 2xTn − Tn−1 works with numbers that all stay in [−1, 1] — stable by construction.
